Your A/B test needs more users than you think

A one-line rule for sample size, and why halving the effect quadruples the cost.

Ryan Kim GitHub LinkedIn

01

The rule

Most A/B tests I’ve seen end the same way: two weeks of traffic, a lift that looks real, and a p-value that won’t drop below 0.05. The usual cause isn’t bad luck. The test was too small to detect the effect it was looking for.

There’s a quick check you can run before you start. For a two-group test at 80% power and α = 0.05, the sample size per group is roughly:

n ≈
16σ2
δ2
(1)

Here σ is the standard deviation of your metric and δ is the smallest difference you care about.1 The δ² in the denominator is the part that hurts. Halve the effect you want to detect and you need four times the users.

Worked example

At a 10% conversion rate, σ² ≈ 0.09. Detecting a lift to 11% takes about 14,400 users per group. Detecting a lift to 10.5% takes about 57,600.

Play with it

The curve below is the chance your test detects a real effect, for each sample size per group. Drag the slider to change the effect size d, the difference divided by σ.

At d = 0.2, which Cohen called a small effect, you need about 400 users per group. At d = 0.1 you need about 1,570.

03

What to do instead

If the number is out of reach, you have three honest options. Test a bigger change, which raises δ. Pick a less noisy metric, which lowers σ. Or wait until you have the traffic. Running the test anyway and hoping is not one of them.
